Part 5 · Torque and Rotational Dynamics

Rotational motion

Every linear quantity has a rotational twin, and the analogy is exact. Learn the correspondence and you have learned the unit.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Translate between linear and rotational quantities
  • Compute a moment of inertia, including by integration
  • Apply the rotational form of Newton's second law
  • Use the parallel axis theorem to change axes

Rotation looks like a new subject and is not. Every definition, law and theorem from the first four chapters has an exact rotational counterpart, obtained by one substitution table. Learning the table is faster than relearning mechanics.

Moment of inertia is not mass

I=∑miri2I = \sum m_i r_i^2I=∑mi​ri2​, or for a continuous body

I=∫r2 dmI = \int r^2\,dm I=∫r2dm

The crucial difference from mass: III depends on where the axis is. The same object has different moments of inertia about different axes, so “the moment of inertia of a rod” is not a well-formed phrase without saying about what.

That second line is the derivation for a rod about its end: linear density ML\tfrac{M}{L}LM​, integrate x2x^2x2 from 000 to LLL, and get ML23\tfrac{ML^2}{3}3ML2​.

About its centre instead, the integral runs from −L2-\tfrac{L}{2}−2L​ to L2\tfrac{L}{2}2L​ and gives ML212\tfrac{ML^2}{12}12ML2​ — four times smaller, because mass near the axis contributes barely anything. The r2r^2r2 weighting is why.

Note

The parallel axis theorem connects them: I=Icm+Md2I = I_{\text{cm}} + Md^2I=Icm​+Md2 for an axis a distance ddd from the centre of mass.

Check it here: ML212+M(L2)2=ML212+ML24=ML23\tfrac{ML^2}{12} + M\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{ML^2}{12} + \tfrac{ML^2}{4} = \tfrac{ML^2}{3}12ML2​+M(2L​)2=12ML2​+4ML2​=3ML2​. The two results agree, which is a free check on any moment of inertia you derive.

Torque

τ=rFsin⁡θ\tau = rF\sin\theta τ=rFsinθ

Only the component of force perpendicular to the radius produces torque. Pushing a door towards its hinge does nothing, however hard — sin⁡0=0\sin 0 = 0sin0=0 — and pushing at the far edge perpendicular to the door is maximally effective.

That is why door handles are opposite the hinge, and it is the whole content of the rrr and sin⁡θ\sin\thetasinθ factors.

The rotational second law follows:

∑τ=Iα\sum \tau = I\alpha ∑τ=Iα

Same structure as ∑F=ma\sum F = ma∑F=ma, same method: identify the torques, sum them with sign (conventionally anticlockwise positive), divide by III.

Rolling combines both

An object rolling without slipping has both kinds of kinetic energy:

K=12Mv2+12Iω2K = \tfrac12 Mv^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega^2 K=21​Mv2+21​Iω2

with v=Rωv = R\omegav=Rω tying them together. That constraint is what “without slipping” means mathematically.

The consequence is a famous result: released from the same height, objects race down an incline in order of their moment of inertia, regardless of mass or radius. A solid sphere (I=25MR2I = \tfrac25 MR^2I=52​MR2) beats a solid cylinder (12MR2\tfrac12 MR^221​MR2), which beats a hoop (MR2MR^2MR2).

The reason is energy bookkeeping. The same MghMghMgh is available to every object, and whatever goes into rotation is unavailable for translation. A hoop puts all its mass at maximum rrr, so it diverts the most into spin and arrives last.

Pitfall

Mass and radius cancel in that race. Students expect the heavy one or the big one to win, and neither does — only the distribution of mass matters, through the dimensionless coefficient in I=cMR2I = cMR^2I=cMR2.

What comes next

Angular momentum, rotational kinetic energy and their conservation laws are unit 6’s subject — chapter 6.1 takes them up, and this chapter’s III is exactly what they are built on.

The division follows the course itself: unit 5 is torque and rotational dynamics, which is τ=Iα\tau = I\alphaτ=Iα and the moments of inertia it needs. Unit 6 is energy and momentum of rotating systems, which is units 3 and 4 with the rotational substitutions.
