Part 1 · Kinematics

Motion in one dimension

Position, velocity and acceleration are one function and its two derivatives. Once you see that, the kinematic equations stop being things to memorise.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Relate position, velocity and acceleration by differentiation
  • Derive the constant-acceleration equations rather than recalling them
  • Say exactly when those equations do not apply
  • Read motion off a graph and a graph off motion

Physics C is the calculus-based course, and this chapter is where that stops being a label. In the algebra-based course the kinematic equations are five facts to memorise. Here they are consequences of one definition, and you can rebuild them in thirty seconds if you forget them.

Velocity is the derivative of position. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity.

v(t)=dxdta(t)=dvdt=d2xdt2v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} v(t)=dtdx​a(t)=dtdv​=dt2d2x​

That is the entire content of one-dimensional kinematics. Everything else is consequence.

Differentiating a motion

Given a position function, the rest follows mechanically.

x(t)=5t3−2t2+7x(t) = 5t^3 - 2t^2 + 7 x(t)=5t3−2t2+7

So v(t)=15t2−4tv(t) = 15t^2 - 4tv(t)=15t2−4t and a(t)=30t−4a(t) = 30t - 4a(t)=30t−4. Note the constant 777 vanished at the first step: where the object starts has no effect on how it moves. Shift the origin and the velocity is unchanged, which is the derivative telling you something physical rather than algebraic.

Questions this now answers immediately:

  • When is it at rest? Solve v(t)=0v(t) = 0v(t)=0: at t=0t = 0t=0 and t=415t = \tfrac{4}{15}t=154​.
  • When is the acceleration zero? 30t−4=030t - 4 = 030t−4=0, so t=215t = \tfrac{2}{15}t=152​.
  • When does it change direction? When vvv changes sign — which is not the same as v=0v = 0v=0, since vvv can touch zero without crossing it.

Pitfall

Speed is not velocity. Speed is ∣v∣|v|∣v∣, and it can increase while the velocity decreases — an object moving in the −x-x−x direction and speeding up has velocity going from −2-2−2 to −5-5−5, which is decreasing, while its speed goes from 222 to 555.

The reliable test: speed increases when vvv and aaa have the same sign, and decreases when they have opposite signs. Exam questions ask this constantly and it is the one place sign conventions genuinely bite.

Deriving the constant-acceleration equations

When aaa is constant, integrate twice. Nothing is memorised.

Starting from dvdt=a\tfrac{dv}{dt} = adtdv​=a and integrating:

v(t)=v0+atv(t) = v_0 + at v(t)=v0​+at

Integrating again, since dxdt=v\tfrac{dx}{dt} = vdtdx​=v:

x(t)=x0+v0t+12at2x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}a t^2 x(t)=x0​+v0​t+21​at2

The first verified line says the velocity equation really does have constant acceleration aaa; the second says differentiating the position equation returns the velocity equation. Those two checks are the derivation.

The # symbols: line is not decoration. Without it the checker’s algebra library splits v0 into v×0v \times 0v×0 — the number zero — and would happily “prove” a statement about nothing at all.

The third equation, the one without ttt, comes from eliminating ttt between them:

v2=v02+2a Δxv^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x v2=v02​+2aΔx

It is worth having, because problems that give you a distance and ask for a speed would otherwise need solving a quadratic for ttt first.

Undefined behaviour

These three equations are only valid when acceleration is constant.

That condition is stated so often it stops being heard, and then it gets applied to a problem where aaa depends on time, position, or velocity — air resistance, a spring, a rocket losing mass — and the answer is confidently wrong.

If aaa is not constant, go back to the definitions and integrate. That always works; the shortcuts do not.

Those are the two integrations, checked. The constants of integration are v0v_0v0​ and x0x_0x0​ — the initial conditions, which is what a constant of integration always is in physics.

Motion as a graph

The derivative relationship shows up geometrically, and reading it off a graph is a standard exam task:

On a position–time graph means
slope velocity
zero slope at rest
curvature acceleration
On a velocity–time graph means
slope acceleration
area under the curve displacement
area below the axis motion backwards

The velocity at t=0.5t = 0.5t=0.5 is 2(0.5)−3=−22(0.5) - 3 = -22(0.5)−3=−2, and the secant slope converges on exactly that. Negative velocity means moving in the −x-x−x direction, which on this graph is the descending part of the parabola.

Note that the area interpretation belongs to velocity–time graphs, not position–time graphs. Area under a position–time curve has no physical meaning, and confusing the two is a reliable way to lose marks.
