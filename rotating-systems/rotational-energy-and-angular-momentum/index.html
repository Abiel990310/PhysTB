Part 6 · Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems

Rotational energy and angular momentum

Units 3 and 4 again, with every linear quantity replaced by its rotational counterpart. The translation is exact, which means there is almost nothing new to learn and one thing that is genuinely new.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Compute rotational kinetic energy, and combine it with translational
  • Solve rolling problems by energy rather than by force
  • Define angular momentum for a particle and for a rigid body
  • Apply conservation of angular momentum, and say when it holds
  • Explain why a spinning skater speeds up without any torque

Unit 5 built the rotational second law, τ=Iα\tau = I\alphaτ=Iα. This unit does for rotation what units 3 and 4 did for translation: energy and momentum. Almost every result is the linear one with symbols swapped, and setting the two side by side is the fastest way to learn both.

The correspondence is not a mnemonic. Every rotational line is derived from its linear partner by summing over the particles of the body, and the derivations are why III appears where mmm does.

Rotational kinetic energy

A particle at distance rrr from the axis moves at v=ωrv = \omega rv=ωr, so its kinetic energy is 12mω2r2\tfrac12 m\omega^2r^221​mω2r2. Summing over the body and pulling out the common ω\omegaω:

Krot=12(∑miri2)ω2=12Iω2K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12\left(\textstyle\sum m_i r_i^2\right)\omega^2 = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 Krot​=21​(∑mi​ri2​)ω2=21​Iω2

That sum is the moment of inertia — the same III from τ=Iα\tau = I\alphaτ=Iα, arrived at from energy instead of from torque. It appearing in both places is the reason it is worth defining at all.

The integral is work done by a torque as the body speeds up, and it produces the half for the same reason it did for 12mv2\tfrac12 mv^221​mv2 and 12kx2\tfrac12 kx^221​kx2: you are integrating something that grows linearly.

Rolling: both at once

A rolling object has kinetic energy of both kinds, and rolling without slipping ties them together by v=ωRv = \omega Rv=ωR:

K=12mv2+12Iω2=12mv2(1+ImR2)K = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12 mv^2\left(1 + \frac{I}{mR^2}\right) K=21​mv2+21​Iω2=21​mv2(1+mR2I​)

The checked line is the general case with I=cmR2I = cmR^2I=cmR2, where ccc is a pure number set by the shape: 12\tfrac1221​ for a solid cylinder, 25\tfrac2552​ for a solid sphere, 111 for a hoop. So a rolling body’s energy is 12mv2(1+c)\tfrac12 mv^2(1+c)21​mv2(1+c), and the fraction going into rotation is c/(1+c)c/(1+c)c/(1+c) — independent of mass, radius and speed.

Race them down a ramp from rest. Energy conservation gives

mgh=12mv2(1+c)⟹v=2gh1+cmgh = \tfrac12 mv^2(1+c) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+c}} mgh=21​mv2(1+c)⟹v=1+c2gh​​

That line is the check that matters: substituting v2=2gh1+cv^2 = \tfrac{2gh}{1+c}v2=1+c2gh​ back into 12mv2(1+c)\tfrac12 mv^2(1+c)21​mv2(1+c) returns mghmghmgh exactly, which is the energy balance the formula was derived from. Every mmm and RRR has cancelled. The winner depends only on the shape. The sphere (c=0.4c = 0.4c=0.4) beats the cylinder (0.50.50.5) beats the hoop (111), and a large heavy hoop ties with a small light one. The hoop loses because all its mass is at the rim, so the largest share of the energy goes into spinning rather than into moving.

Pitfall

A rolling object does not reach 2gh\sqrt{2gh}2gh​. That is the frictionless sliding answer, and it is the c=0c = 0c=0 limit — an object with no moment of inertia at all.

Applying v=2ghv = \sqrt{2gh}v=2gh​ to anything that rolls is the single most common error in this unit, and it is always an overestimate, because some of the energy went into rotation.

Angular momentum

For a particle, angular momentum about a chosen origin is

L⃗=r⃗×p⃗\vec L = \vec r \times \vec p L=r×p​

and for a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis it collapses to the form worth memorising:

L=IωL = I\omega L=Iω

Newton’s second law has an exact rotational twin:

τ⃗net=dL⃗dt\vec\tau_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec L}{dt} τnet​=dtdL​

so when the net external torque is zero, L⃗\vec LL is constant. That is the conservation law, and like linear momentum it holds even through collisions and explosions, where energy does not.

Note

Angular momentum is defined about a point, and the answer depends on which point you choose. A particle moving in a straight line has nonzero angular momentum about any origin not on that line — L=mvdL = mvdL=mvd, with ddd the perpendicular distance — and it is constant, because nothing is accelerating.

Choosing the origin well is most of the skill. Pick the point about which the unknown forces exert no torque, and they disappear from the equation.

The skater

A skater pulls their arms in and spins faster. No torque acts, so L=IωL = I\omegaL=Iω is fixed; halving III doubles ω\omegaω. That much is standard. The part worth noticing is the energy:

K=12Iω2=L22IK = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \frac{L^2}{2I} K=21​Iω2=2IL2​

With LLL fixed and III halved, the kinetic energy doubles. Energy is not conserved here, and it should not be: the skater’s muscles did work pulling their arms inward against the outward push of the rotation. The energy came from them.

Anyone who expects both LLL and KKK to be conserved will find the numbers contradict each other. Only one of them is protected by a conservation law, and which one depends on whether something is doing work.
