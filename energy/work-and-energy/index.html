Part 3 · Work, Energy, and Power

Work and energy

Work is an integral, and the work–energy theorem is what you get when you integrate the second law with respect to distance instead of time.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Compute work as an integral, including for a varying force
  • State and use the work–energy theorem
  • Relate a conservative force to its potential energy by differentiation
  • Read equilibrium and stability off a potential energy curve

Chapter 2 integrated the second law with respect to time and got momentum. Integrate it with respect to distance instead and you get energy. That is the whole relationship between the two, and it explains why energy methods sidestep time entirely — the variable was integrated away.

Work is an integral

W=∫F⃗⋅dx⃗W = \int \vec F \cdot d\vec x W=∫F⋅dx

For a constant force along the motion this collapses to W=FdW = FdW=Fd, which is the algebra-based formula and a special case. The general form is what you need the moment the force varies.

A spring is the standard example. Hooke’s law gives F=−kxF = -kxF=−kx, so stretching from 000 to xxx takes

W=∫0xkx′ dx′=12kx2W = \int_0^x kx' \,dx' = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 W=∫0x​kx′dx′=21​kx2

Both directions are checked: integrating the force gives the energy, and differentiating the energy gives the force back. That reciprocity is the subject of the last section.

Pitfall

The dot product matters. A force perpendicular to the motion does no work.

  • The normal force on a block sliding along a floor: no work.
  • The tension in a string whirling a ball in a circle: no work.
  • Gravity on a satellite in a circular orbit: no work, which is why its speed is constant.

Students often add these into an energy balance and get a wrong answer that looks carefully reasoned.

Conservative forces and potential energy

A force is conservative when the work it does depends only on the endpoints, not the path taken. Gravity and springs are; friction is not.

For those forces, work can be stored as potential energy, defined so that

Fx=−dUdxF_x = -\frac{dU}{dx} Fx​=−dxdU​

The force is the negative gradient of the potential. The minus sign says objects are pushed downhill in potential energy, which is the direction that lowers UUU.

Read those with the minus sign in mind: a spring with U=12kx2U = \tfrac12 kx^2U=21​kx2 gives F=−kxF = -kxF=−kx, restoring toward the origin; gravity with U=mghU = mghU=mgh gives F=−mgF = -mgF=−mg, pointing down.

Reading a potential energy curve

This is a standard Physics C question type, and it is answered entirely by calculus you already have.

Given a graph of U(x)U(x)U(x):

Feature of UUU Physical meaning
slope minus the force
zero slope equilibrium
minimum (U′′>0U'' > 0U′′>0) stable equilibrium
maximum (U′′<0U'' < 0U′′<0) unstable equilibrium
steepness magnitude of the force

The stability rule is just the second derivative test. At a minimum, a small displacement produces a force pushing back toward the minimum; at a maximum, the same displacement produces a force pushing further away.

The equilibria are where U′=0U' = 0U′=0, so x=±1x = \pm 1x=±1. At x=1x = 1x=1 the second derivative 6x6x6x is positive — a minimum, stable. At x=−1x = -1x=−1 it is negative — a maximum, unstable. A marble placed at x=−1x = -1x=−1 stays only if placed perfectly; nudge it and it runs away.

Note

Total energy E=K+UE = K + UE=K+U is constant when only conservative forces act. On a potential curve, that means drawing a horizontal line at height EEE: the particle is confined to the region where U≤EU \le EU≤E, since K=E−UK = E - UK=E−U cannot be negative. The points where the line meets the curve are turning points, where the particle momentarily stops and reverses.
